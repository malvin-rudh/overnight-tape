Debt-cycle read

Applies Ray Dalio's published debt-cycle framework to today's data. Not his view, and not endorsed by him.

The mechanics: Gold competes with cash and bonds as a store of wealth. When the return on bonds after inflation falls, or the dollar loses buying power, holding gold costs less and demand rises. When money tightens, the reverse happens.

What the data say: Three forces. First, inflation is still building: consumer prices are up 3.7% y/y and import prices rose 1.1% in a month. Second, the real 10-year yield slipped to 2.74% from 2.78%, still a high real return but a slightly smaller headwind. Third, breakeven inflation edged up to 2.35%, so expected inflation rose as real yields fell. The template says tightening into a slowdown later turns to easing. Today differs: hiring has stalled at +29k, yet inflation is rising, so markets still price a December hike.

What looks unsustainable: Interest takes about 21% of federal revenue (Q2 2026), like a family whose loan bill grows faster than its pay. Each hike adds to it. These are rough indicators.

What would change this read: US jobless claims at 20:30 HKT Thu 22 Oct, Michigan inflation expectations at 22:00 HKT Fri 23 Oct, and the FOMC decision at 02:00 HKT Thu 29 Oct.

Where this sits in the cycle: Debt keeps growing faster than revenue, now 6.5 times a year's revenue. The big change over the year is real rates: the real 10-year yield went from 2.2 points below real growth to 0.6 points above it, the condition under which debt burdens grow on their own. Fast nominal growth keeps the ratios steady for now. That reads as late in the cycle, in a tightening phase rather than a printing one.

What the framework implies for asset classes: While real yields are high, cash earns a real return. Long-term bonds carry the risk that heavy debts are later eased with printing. Stocks depend on growth holding up under tighter money. Gold has tended to do best in the printing phase, not the tightening phase, so the framework treats it as a diversifier, not a timing call.

AI and tech

Chip stocks fall for an eighth straight day

A stock index is just an average: add up the prices of a group of companies and watch whether the total goes up or down. The Philadelphia Semiconductor Index, which tracks US-listed chip makers, closed Friday at 11,137.29, down 3.83%, its eighth straight daily fall and 11.4% lower on the week, with every member down. Stocktwits said about $2tn of market value vanished over the run, yet the index was still up nearly 60% this year. Outlets blamed hot inflation data, a 10-year Treasury yield near 5.1%, pricier oil, and doubts about borrowing to build AI data centres. Bank of America called the AI trade resilient and said the risk is priced in. Is this a pause or a turn? Nobody knows yet, and the streak comparisons are approximate.

Sources: BigGo Finance · Stocktwits · Yahoo Finance

Investors push back on Nscale's IPO price

An IPO is when a private company sells shares to the public for the first time, and the hard part is agreeing on a price. Nscale, an Nvidia-backed data-centre builder, wants to raise about $3bn on the New York Stock Exchange at a roughly $30bn valuation, but Bloomberg reports investors are pushing back, with some saying $16bn to $19bn is more realistic. Its last private value was $14.6bn. Revenue jumped to $140.6m in early 2026, yet the net loss was $1.02bn. About $103.4bn of future contracts sounds huge, but only about 2.5% was active in late August, and its Norway site is running late. A reported Microsoft withdrawal from a UK deal is unconfirmed. The final price, and whether contracts become revenue, remain unknown.

Sources: Crypto Briefing, citing Bloomberg · TipRanks

Anthropic pledges 5% of its shares to a public benefit fund

Imagine a company setting aside a slice of itself, like a pie, to give away. Anthropic, maker of the Claude AI models, said it will commit 5% of its shares to a new Anthropic Public Benefit Fund, if shareholders approve. The board may add 2% more for each 50% rise in company value above a baseline that has not been disclosed. The fund is a wholly owned company rather than a registered charity, which allows different kinds of giving, such as large donations, direct payments to groups of people, or help with AI's effects on jobs. Its shares cannot fund lobbying or politics. With earlier pledges, about 20% of shares would go to public good. No dollar value was given, and governance details are promised only in the coming months.

Sources: Anthropic · Reuters

Taiwan plans to make unlicensed AI chip exports to China a crime

Today, Taiwan's rules work like a bouncer with a short list of banned names, such as Huawei and SMIC. The plan is to swap that for a rule about the destination: shipping AI servers and advanced chips to China without a licence would become a crime, whoever the buyer is. Economic Affairs Minister Kung Ming-hsin said this will correspond with US measures and give enforcement agencies more legal tools. The backdrop is smuggling: in August, nine people were indicted over 130 AI servers, of which 74 reached China, according to a single source. But this is only a plan. It needs a legal amendment, and there is no timeline, penalty or final product list yet, so how strict it will be is unknown.

Sources: 24/7 Wall St · Startup Fortune · Forkast via Yahoo
