Where this sits in the cycle: The checklist still suggests a highly indebted phase: debt grew relative to output and annual revenue, while foreign Treasury holdings grew more slowly than a year earlier. A household spending more income on interest has less room for other bills. The comparison has limits: the government issues its own currency, and these interest and revenue observations are from different quarters. Negative yield-growth gaps using one annualised quarter do not establish sustainable financing or predict a crisis date.

What the framework implies for asset classes: Positive after-inflation Treasury yields compete with gold. Cash earns short-term interest and loses buying power if prices rise faster. If debt pressure leads to easing that cuts real returns, gold can gain through that channel. Bonds depend on inflation and repayment terms, while stocks depend on productivity and profits outrunning financing costs.

AI and tech

Washington still wants answers from Nvidia

Export compliance means checking that a sale follows the rules governing where a product can go and who can receive it. Think of a warehouse checking a delivery address and its customer’s identity before releasing a restricted item. A company also needs clear responsibility for making those checks, keeping records and handling warning signs. On Sunday, Scott Bessent said Washington still wanted Nvidia to explain China chip sales and identify who controlled compliance. Asking for that explanation is one step in establishing what happened and who made decisions. The inquiry remains unresolved, so whether the sales complied with the rules remains an open question.

Sources: Reuters via MarketScreener · Original interview · 22 Nov interview, cross-checked with original transcript

An upgrade can improve security and still disrupt a service

Replacing an old computer system can interrupt services that depend on it. For example, changing a building’s wiring may briefly cut power even if the new wiring is intended to improve it. On Sunday, US intelligence chief Jay Clayton said Americans should expect further technology interruptions as companies and government agencies replace older systems. Reuters reported that he attributed the recent wave of outages to upgrades and rejected a coordinated or state-sponsored explanation. Those are Clayton’s assessment and forecast. His broad explanation leaves the cause of each individual incident open, and the report provides no independent technical check of every outage.
