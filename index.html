Debt-cycle read

Applies Ray Dalio's published debt-cycle framework to today's data. Not his view, and not endorsed by him.

The mechanics: Gold competes with cash and bonds as a store of wealth. When the return on bonds after inflation rises, holding gold costs more; when inflation outruns yields, or money is printed, gold gains.

What the data say: Three forces. First, the real yield slipped to 2.77% but remains high, a headwind. Second, CPI at 3.74%, up from 3.40%, erodes the buying power of cash, which is supportive. Third, payrolls of only +29k signal slowing. The usual template has slowing growth bring easier money, which helps gold. Today differs: inflation, not growth, is setting policy, so a slowdown arrives with hike pricing rather than cuts.

What looks unsustainable: Real rates near 2.8% against a government owing 6.52 times its annual revenue (Q2 2026), like a family borrowing heavily while its loan rate climbs. These are rough gauges, not a timing tool.

What would change this read: Jobless claims at 20:30 HKT Thu 22 Oct; US flash PMIs at 21:45 HKT Fri 23 Oct; the FOMC decision at 02:00 HKT Thu 29 Oct.

Where this sits in the cycle: Debt is large relative to income and its revenue multiple rose over the year, while the interest share held near 21%. That fits a late, highly indebted phase with limited fiscal room, not a dated crisis. The real-rate gap turned positive over the year, which means borrowing now costs more than real growth delivers; it rests on one noisy quarter of growth.

What the framework implies for asset classes: Cash protects buying power only while its return beats inflation; long bonds stay exposed to shifts in real yields and inflation. Stocks depend on productivity and earnings as well as financing costs. Gold tends to gain when real returns fall or currency credibility weakens, and today's high real yield works against it. These describe pressures, not recommended holdings.

AI and tech

SpaceX's AI unit drives a 161% revenue jump

A quarterly report is just a scoreboard: money in, money out, and where it came from. SpaceX's third quarter shows revenue of $14.575bn, up 161% from $5.576bn a year earlier, and the biggest slice is now its AI segment at $7.56bn, up 830%, ahead of Starlink connectivity at $5.248bn and space at $1.767bn. Net income was $4.4bn against a $1.1bn loss before. The catch is cost: capital spending, meaning money put into equipment, hit $19.6bn, with $16.4bn going to AI. How solid is the profit? One outlet, TechCrunch, says about $1bn of revenue came from a Bitcoin recovery and related-party income, and no analyst expectations or share-price reaction were found.

Sources: SpaceX filing · TechCrunch

Tesla and SpaceX boards study a merger

A merger means two companies becoming one, and nothing here is agreed yet. On SpaceX's results call, Elon Musk said the boards of Tesla and SpaceX have each formed special committees of independent directors to review a possible merger, after shareholders asked. Musk is excluded as "a conflicted party". He called it "a good idea" but not a certainty, citing AI software, chip design, robotics and orbital data centres. A Tesla deal would need approval from 50% of all Tesla shares, not only those that vote, which he called "quite a high hurdle". The Verge, a single source, puts a combined value at "upwards of $3.6 trillion". No terms exist, so the outcome is open.

Sources: The Verge · TechCrunch

US removes DeepSeek from federal civilian systems

DeepSeek is a Chinese AI company, and this US Homeland Security order is a shopping ban plus a cleanup: civilian federal agencies may not buy its products and must remove them, along with third-party products built using DeepSeek. The BBC says it covers agencies such as Treasury, State and Justice, not defence, and took effect at the end of July, so October is when it became public. TechCrunch says DHS warned that data from government users could be accessible to Chinese authorities. That is a stated risk, and no actual misuse is described. Agencies can request exceptions. China's foreign ministry opposed "overstretching the concept of national security".

Sources: Federal Register · BBC · TechCrunch

Google opens TorchTPU to loosen Nvidia's grip

Think of PyTorch as a common language for writing AI programs, and each AI chip as a machine that needs a translator to understand it. TorchTPU is such a translator: on 20 Oct Google open-sourced it so PyTorch programs run natively on its own TPU chips, with existing code needing "minimal changes", and says Meta's PyTorch team co-developed it. Amazon released TorchNeuron for its Trainium chips on 19 Oct. Google claims training was validated on up to 8,000 TPUs with near-linear scaling, meaning twice the chips gives close to twice the speed. Those numbers are Google's own, and TechCrunch, which frames both tools as challenges to Nvidia, notes developers are unlikely to leave it en masse.

Sources: Google Cloud · TechCrunch
