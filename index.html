Debt-cycle read

Applies Ray Dalio's published debt-cycle framework to today's data. Not his view, and not endorsed by him.

The mechanics: When spending financed by money and credit grows faster than the goods and services sold, prices rise. Gold pays no interest, so higher bond yields after inflation increase the cost of holding it.

What the data say: Three forces matter. October consumer prices rose 0.4% on the month, keeping pressure on policy. The 10-year real yield climbed to 2.78% on 10 Nov, increasing the buying power offered by bonds. Breakeven inflation eased to 2.20%, so the rise in nominal yields did not come from higher 10-year inflation compensation. In a debt-monetisation episode real returns can fall. Here they rose, which helps explain why gold fell on an inflation surprise.

What looks unsustainable: Persistent debt growth above revenue growth squeezes future spending. Spending cuts, restructuring, transfers and monetary expansion can each shift the cost of adjustment.

What would change this read: US producer prices at 21:30 HKT Friday could change expectations for inflation and the Fed. Wednesday’s bond holiday delays the next full Treasury session.

Where this sits in the cycle: The checklist still suggests a highly indebted phase: debt grew relative to output and annual revenue, while foreign Treasury holdings grew more slowly than a year earlier. A household spending more income on interest has less room for other bills. The comparison has limits: the government issues its own currency, and these interest and revenue observations are from different quarters. Negative yield-growth gaps using one annualised quarter do not establish sustainable financing or predict a crisis date.

What the framework implies for asset classes: Positive after-inflation Treasury yields compete with gold. If debt pressures eventually induce monetary easing and reduce those returns, gold can gain through that channel. Bonds also depend on inflation and repayment terms; nominal coupons do not guarantee buying power. Stocks depend on productivity and profits outrunning financing costs, which the current labour and output data alone cannot settle.

AI and tech

TSMC approves stakes in two AI developers

Buying shares gives a company an ownership stake in another business. Taiwan chipmaker TSMC’s board authorised two potential investments on November 10, with up to US$500 million for Anthropic common stock at its proposed public offering price and up to US$500 million for OpenAI preferred stock, shares with different rights from common shares. Think of approving a budget before making a purchase. The US$1 billion combined ceiling says how much TSMC can spend, while the amount it has actually invested remains unknown. Anthropic began presenting its proposed stock offering to investors that day, and its public listing was still pending.

Sources: TSMC board resolutions · Reuters · Anthropic roadshow · 10 Nov

Rackspace plans to run AI servers for Together AI

When someone asks an AI model a question, computers have to run the trained model to produce the answer. That work is called inference. Think of a shop serving customers from a kitchen that another business equips and runs. Under an initial multiyear arrangement announced on November 10, Rackspace expects to operate dedicated Nvidia GB300 NVL72 systems for Together AI to handle those requests. Rackspace plans to supply the networking, electricity and cooling that keep the computers connected and running. Deployment is planned for 2027 and depends on conditions being met, so when the systems will actually begin serving requests remains uncertain.

Sources: Joint announcement · 10 Nov
