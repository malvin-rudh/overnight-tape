Debt-cycle read

Applies Ray Dalio's published debt-cycle framework to today's data. Not his view, and not endorsed by him.

The mechanics: When spending financed by money and credit grows faster than the amount sold, prices rise. Gold pays no interest, so higher after-inflation bond yields increase the return an investor gives up by holding it.

What the data say: Three forces matter. First, weekly jobless claims rose to 200,000 from a revised 191,000 on 12 Nov, a softer labour reading. Second, the 10-year real yield rose to 2.86% on 12 Nov, lifting the return available from bonds after inflation. Third, 10-year inflation compensation rose to 2.24% on 12 Nov. When inflation expectations rise faster than nominal yields, real yields can fall. This time real yields rose, so bond returns after expected inflation increased.

What looks unsustainable: If federal debt keeps growing faster than revenue, servicing it absorbs money that could go to other spending. Cuts, restructuring, transfers and monetary expansion spread the adjustment differently.

What would change this read: US producer prices on Friday at 21:30 HKT could change the picture if they shift inflation expectations enough to reverse the rise in real yields.

Where this sits in the cycle: The checklist still suggests a highly indebted phase: debt grew relative to output and annual revenue, while foreign Treasury holdings grew more slowly than a year earlier. A household spending more income on interest has less room for other bills. The comparison has limits: the government issues its own currency, and these interest and revenue observations are from different quarters. Negative yield-growth gaps using one annualised quarter do not establish sustainable financing or predict a crisis date.

What the framework implies for asset classes: Positive after-inflation Treasury yields compete with gold. Cash earns short-term interest and loses buying power if prices rise faster. If debt pressure leads to easing that cuts real returns, gold can gain through that channel. Bonds depend on inflation and repayment terms, while stocks depend on productivity and profits outrunning financing costs.

AI and tech

Nvidia names an interim CEO

Nvidia designs chips that help computers train AI models and run them after training. When a model answers a question, for example, chips do the calculations needed to produce the reply. On November 12, Nvidia announced that Jensen Huang resigned as president, chief executive and board director, effective immediately. Colette Kress, the company’s finance chief, took over as interim chief executive, meaning she leads the company while the board searches for a permanent successor. The board’s choice is still open, and the announcement leaves Nvidia’s future strategy under its next leader unclear.

Sources: Nvidia · AP · 12 Nov US

Cisco raises its full-year revenue forecast

When several computers work together on an AI task, they need to pass information between them. For example, one machine may need another machine’s results before it can finish its own calculations. Cisco sells equipment that helps computers in data centres exchange that information. On November 12, Cisco reported first quarter revenue of US$18.5 billion, up 24% from a year earlier. Reuters cited strong demand for AI networking and cybersecurity equipment. Cisco also raised its forecast for fiscal 2027 revenue to US$74.8 billion to US$75.8 billion from US$72.2 billion to US$73.4 billion. The full year result remains uncertain.

Sources: Cisco · Reuters · 12 Nov US
