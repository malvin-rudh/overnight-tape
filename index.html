Debt-cycle read

Applies Ray Dalio's published debt-cycle framework to today's data. Not his view, and not endorsed by him.

The mechanics: Prices rise when money and credit spent grow faster than output. Gold pays no income, so lower after-inflation bond returns usually improve its relative appeal.

What the data say: Three forces matter. First, the 20 Nov real yield fell to 2.77% from 2.88%, reducing that income disadvantage. Second, inflation compensation slipped to 2.15% from 2.16%. Third, claims were 203,000 for the week to 14 Nov, not a sudden labour collapse. Unlike the usual template, gold fell despite lower real yields. Reported liquidation is a possible missing force, not something these macro readings prove.

What looks unsustainable: Debt persistently outgrowing revenue requires adjustment through cuts, restructuring, transfers or monetary expansion.

What would change this read: After this window, US October PCE and second-estimate Q3 GDP arrive Wednesday 25 Nov at 21:30 HKT. Persistent inflation or weaker real growth would change the balance.

Where this sits in the cycle: The checklist still suggests a highly indebted phase: debt grew relative to output and annual revenue, while foreign Treasury holdings grew more slowly than a year earlier. A household spending more income on interest has less room for other bills. The comparison has limits: the government issues its own currency, and these interest and revenue observations are from different quarters. Negative yield-growth gaps using one annualised quarter do not establish sustainable financing or predict a crisis date.

What the framework implies for asset classes: Positive after-inflation Treasury yields compete with gold. Cash earns short-term interest and loses buying power if prices rise faster. If debt pressure leads to easing that cuts real returns, gold can gain through that channel. Bonds depend on inflation and repayment terms, while stocks depend on productivity and profits outrunning financing costs.

AI and tech

Independent checkers endorse a Claude-assisted Riemann proof

A mathematical proof must connect its starting assumptions to its conclusion through valid logical steps. A computer can check those steps when they are written in a precise language. Levent Alpöge announced a Claude-assisted proof of the Riemann hypothesis, which concerns the distribution of prime numbers, on November 20. He released it as Lean code. On November 21, the Lean and Mathlib communities certified both the theorem statement and the proof. Independent tools matched the statement against the established version, and multiple checkers accepted the proof. Mathematicians still have work to do explaining its new ideas so people can understand how the argument succeeds.

Sources: Lean and Mathlib · Alpöge announcement · 21 Nov verification; 20 Nov announcement

A smaller model reuses its middle layers

An AI model processes text through a sequence of calculation layers. Thinking Machines Lab’s Inkling 2 Mini runs its middle layers three times for each piece of output, like working through the same worksheet several times before answering. Reusing the layers avoids storing three separate copies. Released with open weights on November 20, it has 26.4 billion parameters and uses roughly 20GB of memory. Running it locally on a Mac requires at least 32GB of memory at launch. Its developer says repeated passes improve reasoning efficiency, while stored factual knowledge remains limited by model size. Performance across real tasks remains an open question.

Sources: Thinking Machines · Model card · 20 Nov announcement

Signal blocks more AI screen capture

A private message has to become readable on a screen before its recipient can read it. That creates another place where software could capture it, even when the message was protected during delivery. Signal announced screenshot blocking for iOS 27 on November 20, alongside protection on Android versions where it can detect AI assistants reading the screen. The aim is to stop operating-system AI agents accessing displayed conversations. Users can turn protection off to take screenshots, potentially exposing messages. Signal says coverage remains incomplete, with file access still possible, some Android devices uncovered and reliable screenshot blocking unavailable on macOS.

Sources: Signal · 20 Nov announcement
