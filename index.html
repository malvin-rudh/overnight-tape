Debt-cycle read

Applies Ray Dalio's published debt-cycle framework to today's data. Not his view, and not endorsed by him.

The mechanics: Gold competes with cash and bonds. When bonds pay less after inflation, holding gold gives up less income. Prices tend to rise when spending, including credit, grows faster than the goods and services available to buy.

What the data say: Three forces matter. The latest real yield dipped to 2.64%, easing the cost of holding gold at the margin. The breakeven fell to 2.24%, suggesting less expected inflation. Claims fell to 189,000, a sign layoffs remained low that complicates the case for easier policy. Real yields remain positive and the latest reading predates the sell-off, so the move does not establish a switch to easy money.

What looks unsustainable: When debt grows persistently faster than income, servicing it absorbs more receipts. Spending restraint, restructuring, transfers or monetary easing can shift that burden.

What would change this read: Friday’s US payrolls at 21:30 HKT will test whether hiring is picking up. A stronger jobs and wage print could revive hike pricing, while weaker hiring could reinforce the move toward lower yields.

Where this sits in the cycle: Higher debt relative to revenue and slower growth in foreign Treasury holdings point to a highly indebted phase. A family with a large interest bill has less cash for other expenses even if its income still grows. Here, the yield-growth gaps are still negative on the reported inputs, and the interest share combines different quarters. That is a narrower claim than saying the government already faces an immediate funding crisis.

What the framework implies for asset classes: Positive after-inflation cash and bond yields raise the cost of holding gold. If policy later lowers those real returns to relieve debt pressure, gold can benefit through that channel. Stocks depend on whether productivity and profits grow enough to offset financing costs. Falling yields during a sell-off can reflect weaker expectations for earnings, so the same change can have different effects on bonds and stocks.

AI and tech

Arm earns twice from a chip design

Arm can earn money from the same chip design in two ways. A company pays a license fee to use the design, then pays Arm a royalty when it ships chips based on that design. Think of paying for access to a blueprint, then paying a fee for each product built from it. Arm reported quarterly revenue of $1.44 billion, up 27% from a year earlier, and said its data centre royalties doubled. It also plans to sell its own Arm AGI CPU, the main processor in a computer. Arm estimates up to $2 billion in sales from that chip over two fiscal years, depending on demand and supply.

OpenAI separates image planning from detail

Making an image from a request involves deciding what goes where and then drawing it. Asking for a different shirt colour is a small edit. Asking for three people in specific poses with different props gives the model more constraints to juggle. OpenAI says Images 2.6 is available in ChatGPT and Codex, and developers can use a complexity setting to adjust how much planning the model does. A separate quality setting controls visual detail. Extra planning may help with crowded scenes or precise layouts, but how reliably the model follows every instruction remains open without independent testing.
