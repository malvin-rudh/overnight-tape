Debt-cycle read

Applies Ray Dalio's published debt-cycle framework to today's data. Not his view, and not endorsed by him.

The mechanics: Gold competes with cash and bonds as a store of wealth. Holding it costs the real yield forgone, so it gains when real yields fall or money is printed, and loses when money stays tight.

What the data say: Three forces. First, the real yield fell to 2.64% from 2.76%, cutting gold's holding cost. Second, CPI at 3.7% keeps inflation above target, so policy stays tight. Third, claims of 194k let the Fed focus on prices. The template: a lower real yield helps gold. Today differs: spot gold fell 2.76% anyway (Kitco, 23 Oct 07:16 HKT). The template misses a drop that size; selling across metals may be the missing influence.

What looks unsustainable: Federal debt at 6.52 times a year's revenue, with interest taking 21.1% of revenue: a family whose loan bill eats a fifth of its pay. Rough gauges, not timing tools.

What would change this read: a hike or hawkish guidance at the FOMC decision, 02:00 HKT Thu 29 Oct, that lifts the real yield back above 2.76%; or core PCE above 3.0% in the September data at 20:30 HKT Thu 29 Oct.

Where this sits in the cycle: Debt is still growing faster than the income that services it: 6.52 times a year's revenue against 6.40 a year earlier, and 122.6% of GDP. The biggest change over the year is the real borrowing cost, now 0.56 points above real growth after sitting 2.30 points below; when that gap is positive, debt burdens compound on their own. Nominal growth still outpaces the nominal 10-year yield, a cushion from inflation rather than output. Foreign buying has slowed to +3.4% from +11.6%, and the Fed's balance sheet is growing again. This reads as a late, highly indebted phase, not a dated turning point.

What the framework implies for asset classes: Cash protects buying power only while its return beats inflation; long bonds carry the risk that debts are eased away through inflation. Stocks depend on productivity and earnings as well as financing costs. Gold tends to gain when real returns fall or currency credibility weakens, while today's high real yields still work against it. These describe pressures, not recommended holdings.

AI and tech

Lam's supply warning hits chip stocks

Lam Research sells the machines that chipmakers use to build chips, a bit like a company selling ovens to bakeries: if the ovens arrive late, the bread does too. Lam said its revenue guidance would have been a bit higher without near-term supply chain constraints, and its CFO expects shipments slipping out of the December quarter to ship in the March quarter. The CEO said clean room space and certain components will keep limiting chipmakers into calendar 2027, yet also said the company would be "caught up very shortly" and that this is "nothing like" COVID-era shortages. Those two messages fit awkwardly together. The Philadelphia Semiconductor Index fell 6.7% on Thursday, but Meta spending and oil were also cited, so Lam's role is not proven.

Sources: 24/7 Wall St. via Yahoo Finance · Reuters

Meta's AI spending turns its cash flow negative

Free cash flow is what is left after a company pays for running the business and for building things, like a paycheck minus the mortgage and the renovation bills. Meta's was negative $2.1bn in the third quarter of 2026, its first negative quarter since 2012. The cause is capital spending, or capex, on things like data centres: $34.9bn this quarter against $19.4bn a year earlier. Meta set 2026 capex guidance at $135bn to $145bn and gave no 2027 number. A filing showed about $825bn of future spending commitments, as reported by one outlet. Shares fell about 7%. Does a negative quarter mean trouble? Not by itself. The open question is when the spending earns its cost back.

Sources: investingLive · Reuters

Nvidia and Tesla reportedly fund Samsung chip substrates

An AI chip cannot sit directly on a circuit board. It is mounted on a base layer called an FC-BGA substrate, a bit like an adapter plate that connects the chip to the board. Reuters, citing a South Korean newspaper and unnamed industry sources, reported that Samsung Electro-Mechanics will receive 3.7 trillion won, about $2.8bn, with 2.6 trillion from Nvidia and 1.1 trillion from Tesla. The money would build production lines in Sejong under a long-term supply contract. No company confirmed the amounts. Some reports call this circular financing, which simply means customers funding their own suppliers. Whether that is a worry or sensible planning, and what the final amounts and terms are, remains unknown.

Sources: Reuters via Investing.com (single press report)

CoreWeave's debt costs climb

CoreWeave rents out AI computing power and has borrowed heavily to build it, a bit like a landlord with a very large mortgage. Its bonds were issued in June 2026 at a 9.675% yield and traded around 15% in the week to 19 October. A higher yield means lenders demand more for the same loan because they see more risk of not being repaid. Shares fell about 10% on Thursday, and HSBC repeated a "Reduce" rating. One analyst estimated 5% to 10% of planned AI infrastructure projects may not proceed, but that is an estimate, not a confirmed figure. Deutsche Bank's Jim Reid said AI financing costs "aren't dramatic overall". Whether this is an overreaction or the start of tighter financing is unknown.

Sources: Yahoo Finance · FX Leaders
