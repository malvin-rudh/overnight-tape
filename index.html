Debt-cycle read

Applies Ray Dalio's published debt-cycle framework to today's data. Not his view, and not endorsed by him.

The mechanics: Inflation can rise when spending financed by money and credit outpaces output. Gold pays no interest, so higher bond returns after inflation increase the cost of holding it instead of an income-producing asset.

What the data say: Three forces matter. The 23 Nov real yield rose from 2.77% to 2.79%, increasing that income disadvantage. Market inflation compensation fell to 2.13% from 2.15%, while nominal yields held steady. The broad dollar index rose from 123.5253 to 123.5978 on 20 Nov, an older input consistent with pressure on buyers using other currencies. Gold still recovered. Reuters reported buying after last week’s drop, a flow the rates and currency data cannot measure.

What looks unsustainable: Debt cannot keep growing faster than revenue indefinitely. Spending cuts, restructuring, transfers and monetary expansion shift the burden in different ways.

What would change this read: October PCE and the second estimate of Q3 GDP arrive 25 Nov at 21:30 HKT. If inflation cools while growth slows, expected tightening could ease. Persistent inflation could sustain positive real returns.

Where this sits in the cycle: The checklist still suggests a highly indebted phase: debt grew relative to output and annual revenue, while foreign Treasury holdings grew more slowly than a year earlier. A household spending more income on interest has less room for other bills. The comparison has limits: the government issues its own currency, and these interest and revenue observations are from different quarters. Negative yield-growth gaps using one annualised quarter do not establish sustainable financing or predict a crisis date.

What the framework implies for asset classes: Positive after-inflation Treasury yields compete with gold. Cash earns short-term interest and loses buying power if prices rise faster. If debt pressure leads to easing that cuts real returns, gold can gain through that channel. Bonds depend on inflation and repayment terms, while stocks depend on productivity and profits outrunning financing costs.

Nvidia reinstates Jensen Huang

A company’s board chooses its leadership, while an investigation examines what happened and whether responsibilities were met. Think of appointing someone to run a workplace while a separate review continues checking its records. Nvidia announced on November 23 that its board unanimously voted to reinstate Jensen Huang as president, chief executive and director, effective immediately. Colette Kress remains finance chief. The independent board committee’s investigation continues, and Nvidia says it will keep cooperating with government investigations while seeking a chief compliance officer to oversee adherence to rules. What those investigations will conclude remains open, even with the leadership appointment settled.

Applied Digital closes construction financing

A construction loan gives a company money to keep building before it arranges longer term funding. Think of paying for the next stages of a house while financing the rest is still being organised. Applied Digital said on November 23 that it closed a US$300 million loan for its Delta Forge 1 AI data centre campus in Louisiana. Money becomes available subject to conditions, including construction milestones and approvals, and the loan matures in 364 days. The company says the campus has a long term lease with a large US computing provider. Funding to finish construction remains open, with more financing expected.
