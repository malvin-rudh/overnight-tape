Debt-cycle read

Applies Ray Dalio's published debt-cycle framework to today's data. Not his view, and not endorsed by him.

The mechanics: When money and credit spent grow faster than the quantity sold, prices rise. A bond’s yield after inflation is the return forgone by holding gold, which pays no interest.

What the data say: Three forces set the context. October core PCE inflation was 3.3% year on year, limiting room to ease. The 10-year real yield rose to 2.79% on 27 Nov, increasing gold’s opportunity cost. The 10-year breakeven rose to 2.15% on 27 Nov, signalling slightly greater inflation compensation in bond prices. Gold still rebounded. Reuters reported bargain buying after the week’s low, a source of demand the yield comparison alone misses.

What looks unsustainable: If interest consumes a rising share of public revenue, it squeezes the rest of the budget. Smaller deficits, debt restructuring, transfers and money creation distribute the burden in different ways.

What would change this read: OPEC’s 29 Nov meeting could shift the energy outlook. The hour is unconfirmed. The next US payroll report is due 4 Dec at 21:30 HKT. A material surprise could move real yields.

Where this sits in the cycle: Debt remains high relative to annual receipts, with the ratio up from a year earlier on this measure. A household with a larger interest bill has less room for other spending, while a government that issues its own currency has different financing options. The revised growth estimate widened the negative yield-growth gaps, but a single quarter of annualised growth is a noisy guide to long-run debt service.

What the framework implies for asset classes: Positive after-inflation Treasury yields compete with gold. Cash earns short-term interest and loses buying power if prices rise faster. If debt pressure leads to easing that cuts real returns, gold can gain through that channel. Bonds depend on inflation and repayment terms, while stocks depend on productivity and profits outrunning financing costs.

AI and tech

JERA’s Tokyo-area data centre talks

A data centre needs computers and a steady supply of electricity. Putting it beside a power plant is like placing a workshop close to its energy supply. On November 27, Reuters relayed Nikkei’s report that JERA was discussing Tokyo-area data centres with Anthropic and Meta. JERA confirmed its partner RHAELM was talking to several large cloud companies, leaving the specific named-company talks attributed to Nikkei. An October statement of intent outlines AI facilities starting in Chiba, using power directly from an operating station. JERA’s expected investment above US$15 billion and operations around 2028 remain plans, with customer agreements still unconfirmed.

Sources: Reuters citing Nikkei · JERA October announcement

Canada expands its AI computing fund

Building AI can mean paying to use someone else’s computers while a model learns or answers requests. A computing subsidy helps with that bill, much as a grant might cover part of a workshop’s equipment rental. Canada announced on November 26 that it was adding C$700 million to its AI Compute Access Fund, bringing it to C$1 billion for small and medium Canadian companies. The guide allows support for up to 80% of eligible sovereign cloud costs and 50% of eligible Canadian-based cloud costs. Only qualifying spending is covered, and whether funded projects produce successful AI products remains open.

Sources: Government announcement · Program guide
