Debt-cycle read

Applies Ray Dalio's published debt-cycle framework to today's data. Not his view, and not endorsed by him.

The mechanics: Gold competes with cash and bonds as a place to keep wealth. When they pay more than inflation, holding gold costs that real return. When inflation outruns yields, or debts look likely to be inflated away with printed money, gold gains.

What the data say: Three forces pull different ways. First, the real return on bonds is high: the 10-year real yield is 2.88%, a headwind. Second, inflation is sticky at 3.4% CPI and 3.0% core PCE, so markets price about a 90% chance of a hike by 9 December (Fed Rate Monitor, 11:15 HKT). Third, jobs are cooling: payrolls rose 29k in September after 133k. The usual template is that tightening into a slowing economy ends in easing, which helps gold. Today differs because inflation has not turned down yet.

What looks unsustainable: Interest takes about 21% of federal revenue, like a household spending a fifth of its pay on loan interest while its rate keeps rising. These are rough indicators.

What would change this read: US CPI tonight at 20:30 HKT; a soft print would weaken the hike case. Then retail sales and PPI, Thu 20:30.

Where this sits in the cycle: Debt keeps growing faster than revenue, now 6.5 times a year's revenue. The big change is real rates: the real 10-year yield went from 2.2 points below real growth to 0.7 points above it, the condition under which debt burdens grow on their own. Fast nominal growth keeps the ratios steady for now. That reads as late in the cycle, in a tightening phase rather than a printing one.

What the framework implies for asset classes: While real yields are high, cash earns a real return. Long-term bonds carry the risk that heavy debts are later eased with printing. Stocks depend on growth holding up under tighter money. Gold has tended to do best in the printing phase, not the tightening phase, so the framework treats it as a diversifier, not a timing call.

Explained

Where this goes: why the IMF worries when an investor is also its own customer.

Try first: If you lend a friend money so they can buy your bike, how sure are you of getting paid?

The idea: A sale proves demand only if the buyer's money is really theirs. Picture a bakery lending a café cash that the café spends on the bakery's bread. If the café fails, the bakery loses the loan and the customer at once. The IMF counts 14 deals where big cloud companies put about $300bn of equity and $650bn of debt into AI firms that promised about $1.4tn of future spending, mostly on cloud and chips. Risk managers call this "wrong-way risk": what you are owed gets riskier just as the borrower weakens.

Still unknown: The IMF does not name the 14 deals, so outsiders cannot check which promises are binding.
