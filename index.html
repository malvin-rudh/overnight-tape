Debt-cycle read

Applies Ray Dalio's published debt-cycle framework to today's data. Not his view, and not endorsed by him.

The mechanics: Gold competes with cash and bonds as a store of wealth. Holding it means giving up the real yield, the return after inflation, so a rising real yield raises gold's cost and inflation running above yields lowers it.

What the data say: The template says gold gains when real returns on bonds fall. Three forces today. First, the real yield held at 2.66%, still high. Second, bonds still beat inflation: a 5.00% 10-year against 3.7% CPI. Third, unemployment of 4.2% and 194k claims let the Fed keep money tight. All three lean against gold. The difference from the template: Monday's bounce came through cheaper oil easing inflation fears, not through lower real yields, and the 2.34% breakeven did not move. These are rough gauges.

What looks unsustainable: Federal debt is 6.52 times revenue and interest takes 21.1% of it, like a family whose interest bill eats a fifth of its pay and grows with every rate rise.

What would change this read: An FOMC statement at 02:00 HKT Thu 29 Oct that pushes back on December hikes, or core PCE below the 0.3% m/m forecast (Forex Factory, checked 27 Oct) at 20:30 HKT Thu 29 Oct.

Where this sits in the cycle: Debt is still growing faster than the income that services it: 6.52 times revenue against 6.40 a year ago. The real 10-year yield sits 0.46 points above real growth, against 2.29 points below a year earlier, so the real cost of carrying debt exceeds the real growth that pays for it, a mark of the late, highly indebted phase. Nominal growth still outruns nominal yields, which keeps the burden manageable for now, though one quarter of GDP is a noisy gauge.

What the framework implies for asset classes: In the framework's logic, cash earns a positive real return while policy stays tight; bonds are exposed if inflation stays above 3%; stocks depend on productivity outrunning higher funding costs; and gold tends to benefit if policy makers later ease and let inflation shrink debts, and to suffer while real yields stay high. These are pressures, not holdings.

AI and tech

Anthropic files publicly for its IPO

An S-1 is a disclosure document a company hands the US securities regulator before selling shares to the public, and on Monday Anthropic filed one, planning to list on Nasdaq as ANT. The headline numbers are lopsided: $16.3 billion of revenue in six months, against $4.6 billion in all of 2025. The net loss of $80.7 billion sounds like cash burned, but Yahoo Finance says most of it came from accounting charges, which are bookkeeping entries rather than money spent. The offer size and price range are unset and the date is unconfirmed. About a fifth of revenue came from two customers, though one outlet says 25%, so that share is disputed.

Sources: Yahoo Finance · Analytics Insight · AI Weekly

Tesla jumps on talk of a SpaceX combination

A takeout premium is the extra price a buyer pays over the current share price, and talk of one lifted Tesla shares about 5% on Monday. Yahoo Finance reported that Barclays upgraded Tesla to Overweight and raised its price target to $450 from $370, citing a possible SpaceX combination. Think of it as a bet on a wedding nobody has announced: neither company has confirmed talks, and the Barclays view is a forecast, not a deal in progress. Barclays said on 14 October that an announcement is possible before December. Hurdles include regulatory approval, particularly in China, and governance worries for Tesla's public shareholders.

Sources: Yahoo Finance · InvestingLive
