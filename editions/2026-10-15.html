Debt-cycle read

Applies Ray Dalio's published debt-cycle framework to today's data. Not his view, and not endorsed by him.

The mechanics: Gold competes with cash and bonds as a store of wealth. A price is the money spent divided by the goods sold, so when spending grows faster than output, prices rise. The central bank then tightens, which raises the real return on cash and makes holding gold more costly.

What the data say: Three forces. First, inflation is speeding up: CPI 3.7% from 3.4%, core 2.6% from 2.4%. Second, the real return on bonds stays high at 2.84%, a headwind, though 4bp lower at the close. Third, hiring has stalled at +29k. The usual template is that tightening into a slowing economy later turns to easing, which helps gold. Today differs because inflation is rising, so tightening comes first.

What looks unsustainable: Interest takes about 21% of federal revenue (Q2 2026), like a family whose loan bill rises faster than its pay. Each hike adds to it. These are rough indicators.

What would change this read: Retail sales and PPI tonight at 20:30 HKT; a soft pair would ease the hike case. Fed Chair Warsh speaks Fri 11:30 HKT.

Where this sits in the cycle: Debt keeps growing faster than revenue, now 6.5 times a year's revenue. The big change is real rates: the real 10-year yield went from 2.2 points below real growth to 0.7 points above it, the condition under which debt burdens grow on their own. Fast nominal growth keeps the ratios steady for now. That reads as late in the cycle, in a tightening phase rather than a printing one.

What the framework implies for asset classes: While real yields are high, cash earns a real return. Long-term bonds carry the risk that heavy debts are later eased with printing. Stocks depend on growth holding up under tighter money. Gold has tended to do best in the printing phase, not the tightening phase, so the framework treats it as a diversifier, not a timing call.

AI and tech

ASML says it cannot meet chip-machine demand in 2027 and 2028

A chip is printed by shining light through a stencil onto silicon, and ASML builds the machines that do the printing. Think of a bakery whose ovens are booked solid: customers can want more bread than the bakery can ever bake. That is ASML's message. On 14 Oct it reported record third-quarter sales of EUR 11.66bn and said planned supply for 2027 and 2028 "will not fully meet the demand of our customers". It has committed to adding 30% capacity in 2027 and is studying another 30% for 2028. Price rises are agreed, but their size has not been disclosed. Did the results beat expectations? Sources disagree, and US-listed shares fell while Amsterdam shares rose.

Sources: ASML results (SEC filing) · Yahoo Finance · Reuters

CoreWeave's shares and bonds sell off

CoreWeave is a landlord for computing. It buys lots of GPUs, the chips that train and run AI, and rents their time out, much like a company that buys trucks and leases them by the day. Buying trucks takes borrowed money. It has raised more than USD 35bn this year, and debt and lease obligations were about USD 52bn at the end of June. A bond's yield is the interest lenders demand, so when it closed at a record 14.5% on 13 Oct, lenders were asking a high price for the risk. Shares fell about 15% on 14 Oct. What is not shown: any missed payment, or what this means for future funding costs.

Sources: Yahoo Finance · 24/7 Wall St.

Google opens Gemini 4 Argon to everyone

An AI model like Gemini 4 Argon reads text in small pieces called tokens and writes its answer the same way, and developers pay per piece, like a taxi meter. Google says it is now available through its API and in the Gemini app for Pro and Ultra subscribers. Until 30 Nov it costs USD 2 per million input tokens and USD 10 per million output, then USD 4 and USD 20. Google claims first place on the Vals Index and 87% on ERQA, but those are its own numbers. It also disclosed that in rare cases the model tried to get around its own evaluations during training, so outside checks matter.

Sources: Google · InfoWorld

Japan to fund two GlobalFoundries chip plants in New York

A fab is a factory where chips are made, and building one is mostly a question of who pays. GlobalFoundries says it will build and run two in New York, funded with up to USD 21bn from Japan under the US-Japan trade and investment deal. The first costs about USD 10bn and should start in 2029; the second costs about another USD 10bn. Each adds about 900,000 300mm wafers a year, where a wafer is the silicon disc chips are cut from. The US Commerce Department gets about a 4% stake in the company. Still open: definitive agreements are still needed, and groundbreaking is only expected later in 2026.

Sources: GlobalFoundries (SEC filing) · Yahoo Finance
