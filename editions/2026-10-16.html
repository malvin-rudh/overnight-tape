Debt-cycle read

Applies Ray Dalio's published debt-cycle framework to today's data. Not his view, and not endorsed by him.

The mechanics: Gold competes with cash and bonds as a store of wealth. When the after-inflation return on bonds falls, or the dollar loses buying power, holding gold costs less and demand rises. When money tightens, the reverse happens.

What the data say: Three forces. First, inflation is building in the pipeline: producer prices are up 6.3% y/y, consumer prices 3.7%. Second, the real 10-year yield eased to 2.78% from 2.84%, still a high real return but a smaller headwind, which helped gold today. Third, spending holds up while hiring has stalled at +29k. The template says tightening into a slowdown later turns to easing, which favours gold. Today differs because inflation is still rising, so markets price hikes first.

What looks unsustainable: Interest takes about 21% of federal revenue (Q2 2026), like a family whose loan bill grows faster than its pay. Each hike adds to it. These are rough indicators.

What would change this read: Warsh's remarks at 11:30 HKT today; the FOMC decision at 02:00 HKT on Thu 29 Oct.

Where this sits in the cycle: Debt keeps growing faster than revenue, now 6.5 times a year's revenue. The big change over the year is real rates: the real 10-year yield went from 2.3 points below real growth to 0.6 points above it, the condition under which debt burdens grow on their own. Fast nominal growth keeps the ratios steady for now. That reads as late in the cycle, in a tightening phase rather than a printing one.

What the framework implies for asset classes: While real yields are high, cash earns a real return. Long-term bonds carry the risk that heavy debts are later eased with printing. Stocks depend on growth holding up under tighter money. Gold has tended to do best in the printing phase, not the tightening phase, so the framework treats it as a diversifier, not a timing call.

AI and tech

TSMC posts a record quarter, shares still slip

TSMC is a factory for other companies' chip designs, so it sells manufacturing, not products with its own logo. Think of a bakery that bakes every brand's cakes. Its third-quarter revenue was US$46.72bn, up 50.9% from a year earlier, and net income rose 68.7%. Gross margin, the share of each sale left after production costs, was 66.4%. Why would shares fall after a record? Markets trade on the future: reports said investors focused on softer margin guidance (65% to 67% for the next quarter) and heavy spending. That reason comes from a single source, though, and the figures still await board approval.

Sources: TSMC filing · AP

A Chinese memory claim helps knock 7.6% off Korea's market

A memory chip stores data in tiny cells, each holding one bit, so packing cells tighter means more memory per chip. Most DRAM today uses a cell layout called 6F², and CXMT, China's largest DRAM maker, says it plans server memory modules with a 4F² layout by year-end. Four units of area instead of six could mean roughly 30% to 50% more density, but that is theory, not measured. A launch is also not mass production, and reports say the products may only partly use 4F². Samsung's version is not expected before 2028. On 15 Oct Kospi fell 7.57%, with worries about Chinese supply, AI returns and US inflation all cited, so how much this claim mattered is unclear.

Sources: TrendForce · Korea JoongAng Daily

Anthropic reportedly tells investors of $16.8bn quarterly revenue

Revenue is money coming in; adjusted profit is profit after leaving out some costs, such as stock-based pay. Anthropic, which makes Claude, reportedly told investors at a private meeting that third-quarter revenue was about USD 16.8bn, up about 45% from the second quarter, with USD 1.70 of revenue for each USD 1 spent on computing power. That growth is slower than the second quarter's jump of over 140%, when Claude Code demand surged. The key caveat: none of this comes from a public filing, and it is unverified. It also gave no September annualised rate or full-year outlook, and some investors were disappointed. An IPO is reportedly planned for November, with valuation talk ranging from USD 2 trillion down to under USD 1.5 trillion.

Sources: The Decoder · Yahoo Finance

Nvidia's chief says CoreWeave needs no backstop

CoreWeave is a landlord for computing power: it rents AI companies racks of Nvidia graphics chips. Nvidia owns about 10% of it, supplies its equipment and has helped finance its growth. Critics call that circular financing, meaning money flows round in a loop between supplier and customer. How worried is the market? Insurance against CoreWeave's debt defaulting hit a record 1,194.5 basis points, implying over a 60% chance of default within five years. Nvidia's Jensen Huang said CoreWeave needs no backstop and that its cost of capital is decreasing. He gave no figures, so that remains an open question. Shares fell 15.5% on 14 Oct, then closed up 5.9% on 15 Oct.

Sources: Investing.com via Yahoo Finance · AP
