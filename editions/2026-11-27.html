Debt-cycle read

Applies Ray Dalio's published debt-cycle framework to today's data. Not his view, and not endorsed by him.

The mechanics: When credit and money fund spending that grows faster than output, prices can rise. Gold pays no interest, so higher returns on bonds after inflation raise the cost of holding bullion.

What the data say: Three observations frame the holiday. October core PCE inflation was 3.3% year on year, keeping pressure on policymakers to contain prices. The latest 10-year real yield was 2.73% on 25 Nov. That decline had reduced gold’s opportunity cost before the holiday. Ten-year market inflation compensation held at 2.12% on 25 Nov. The last cash readings predate Thursday’s modest gold decline. Kitco reported a firmer dollar and continued rate-hike expectations during the holiday session.

What looks unsustainable: Interest costs taking an ever-larger share of revenue would leave less room for other public spending. Spending cuts, restructuring, transfers and money creation spread the adjustment differently.

What would change this read: US advance goods trade and inventories are due at 21:30 HKT today. A material surprise could move the dollar as cash markets reopen for a shortened session.

Where this sits in the cycle: Debt remains high relative to annual receipts, with the ratio up from a year earlier on this measure. A household with a larger interest bill has less room for other spending, while a government that issues its own currency has different financing options. The revised growth estimate widened the negative yield-growth gaps, but a single quarter of annualised growth is a noisy guide to long-run debt service.

What the framework implies for asset classes: Positive after-inflation Treasury yields compete with gold. Cash earns short-term interest and loses buying power if prices rise faster. If debt pressure leads to easing that cuts real returns, gold can gain through that channel. Bonds depend on inflation and repayment terms, while stocks depend on productivity and profits outrunning financing costs.

AI and tech

FCC conditionally authorizes SpaceX’s orbital computing project

A computer in orbit could process information and send the results back for use on Earth. Think of putting a remote computing centre on satellites. On November 25, the FCC authorised SpaceX to deploy and operate up to one million proposed Starmind satellites, approving part of its application and leaving the rest for later consideration. The first authorised satellite must be operating in its assigned orbit by November 25, 2033, with further deployment milestones after that. Permission gives SpaceX a route to attempt the project under conditions. Whether it can build the system and operate it economically remains an open question.

Sources: FCC order · Associated Press

Nvidia reportedly pauses some Southeast Asian server shipments

Shipping a computer abroad can require permission when it contains restricted chips. Think of a delivery waiting while the sender checks which permits cover the equipment and its destination. The Financial Times reported on November 26 that Nvidia told manufacturing partners to halt server shipments containing its chips to Malaysia, Singapore, Thailand and Indonesia. Reuters relayed the report and said it could not immediately verify it. Reuters also reported that new US restrictions require export licences for some advanced chips sent to those countries. If the reported pause is confirmed, the affected orders and how long they will wait remain unclear.
