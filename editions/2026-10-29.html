Debt-cycle read

Applies Ray Dalio's published debt-cycle framework to today's data. Not his view, and not endorsed by him.

The mechanics: Gold competes with cash and bonds as a store of wealth. Holding it gives up the real yield, the return after inflation, so a rising real yield makes gold costlier to hold and inflation above yields makes it cheaper.

What the data say: The template says gold loses when real yields rise and money stays tight. Three forces today. First, the real yield rose 3bp to 2.67%. Second, a 5.05% 10-year still beats 3.7% CPI, so bonds pay a real return. Third, 4.2% unemployment and 194k claims let the Fed stay tight, and two officials voted to hike on 28 Oct (Fed). All three lean against gold. The difference: spot held near $4,050, while the breakeven rose 3bp to 2.38%, so a geopolitical bid the template leaves out seems to be at work. Rough gauges.

What looks unsustainable: Federal debt is 6.52 times revenue and interest takes 21.1% of it, like a family whose interest bill eats a fifth of its pay and grows with every rate rise.

What would change this read: September core PCE below the 0.3% m/m forecast, or Q3 GDP well under the 3.2% forecast (Forex Factory), both at 20:30 HKT today.

Where this sits in the cycle: Debt is still growing faster than the income that services it: 6.52 times revenue against 6.40 a year ago. The real 10-year yield sits 0.44 points above real growth, against 2.27 points below a year earlier, so the real cost of carrying debt now exceeds the real growth that pays for it, a mark of the late, highly indebted phase. Nominal growth still outruns nominal yields, which keeps the burden manageable for now, though one quarter of GDP is a noisy gauge.

What the framework implies for asset classes: In the framework's logic, cash earns a positive real return while policy stays tight; bonds are exposed if inflation stays above 3%; stocks depend on productivity outrunning higher funding costs; and gold tends to benefit if policy makers later ease and let inflation shrink debts, and to suffer while real yields stay high. These are pressures, not holdings.

AI and tech

Alphabet's cloud doubles, but AI spending pushes cash flow negative

Think of a company that suddenly builds a huge number of factories before knowing the orders will fill them. Alphabet spent $51.4 billion on property and equipment in the quarter, against $24.0 billion a year earlier, driven by AI. Meanwhile Google Cloud revenue rose 103% to $30.7 billion, so customers are buying. Yet operating cash flow of $42.3 billion minus that spending left free cash flow at negative $9.1 billion, meaning more cash went out than came in. Net income fell to $26.2 billion from $35.0 billion, partly from an $8.7 billion paper loss on equity securities. Will the spending earn its cost back? That is unknown, and shares fell about 10% after hours.

Sources: Alphabet SEC filing · Investing.com · Fortune

Microsoft's Azure grows 48% as capital spending doubles

Azure is Microsoft's rental service for computing power: a company pays to use Microsoft's servers instead of buying its own. Azure revenue grew 48% to $33.165 billion, inside total revenue of $92.3 billion, up 19%. Meanwhile Microsoft spent $39.4 billion on property and equipment, double the $19.4 billion a year earlier. Analysts had expected about $46 billion, so the figure came in roughly $6 billion lower. Why? The quarterly number alone cannot say whether it reflects delivery timing, construction or supply limits, and that remains unknown. Non-GAAP earnings per share of $4.89 leave out the effect of its OpenAI investment, and shares rose about 4% after hours.

Sources: Microsoft · investingLive · Yahoo Finance

Oracle promises $225 billion of revenue by fiscal 2030 and caps new funding

A target is a promise about the future, not a result. Oracle said it aims for at least $225 billion of revenue in fiscal 2030, which implies growth above 35% a year from fiscal 2026, with a 40% or higher operating margin. It also expects capital spending to peak in fiscal 2027 to 2028 and free cash flow, the cash left after spending, to turn positive in the second half of fiscal 2028. The catch: new stock and debt raising is capped at $20 billion through May 2030. How does the AI data-centre build get paid for inside that cap? Unanswered. Shares closed up 10.73% at $144.54, though analysts already expected $224.2 billion.

Sources: Oracle release via StockTitan · CNBC · Motley Fool
