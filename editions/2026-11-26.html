Debt-cycle read

Applies Ray Dalio's published debt-cycle framework to today's data. Not his view, and not endorsed by him.

The mechanics: When spending financed by money and credit outpaces production, more money competes for each unit of output. Gold pays no interest, so a fall in the return on Treasury bonds after inflation reduces the cost of holding it.

What the data say: Three forces help explain the overnight move. October core PCE inflation was 3.3% year on year, keeping pressure on policymakers to contain prices. The 10-year real yield fell to 2.73% from 2.79% on 25 Nov, reducing gold’s opportunity cost. Ten-year market inflation compensation held at 2.12%. That points to little change in the inflation premium investors priced into longer-term bonds. The early sell-off and later recovery show how inflation news and shifts in rates can weigh on the same market at different points in the session.

What looks unsustainable: Interest and debt service cannot indefinitely grow faster than the revenue available to pay them. Spending cuts, restructuring, transfers and money creation change who bears that cost.

What would change this read: The ECB publishes its October policy account at 20:30 HKT today. A surprise on the rate outlook could move the euro and dollar during the US Thanksgiving holiday.

Where this sits in the cycle: Debt remains high relative to annual receipts, with the ratio up from a year earlier on this measure. A household with a larger interest bill has less room for other spending, while a government that issues its own currency has different financing options. The revised growth estimate widened the negative yield-growth gaps, but a single quarter of annualised growth is a noisy guide to long-run debt service.

What the framework implies for asset classes: Positive after-inflation Treasury yields compete with gold. Cash earns short-term interest and loses buying power if prices rise faster. If debt pressure leads to easing that cuts real returns, gold can gain through that channel. Bonds depend on inflation and repayment terms, while stocks depend on productivity and profits outrunning financing costs.

AI and tech

HP says AI PCs made up nearly 60% of shipments

An AI PC is a computer equipped to run AI features. Selling one records a purchase, while learning whether those features help requires seeing what buyers actually do with them. HP’s interim chief executive said these computers accounted for nearly 60% of shipments in its latest quarter, reported on November 24. Its Personal Systems business kept US$3 in operating profit for every US$100 of sales, down from US$5.80 a year earlier. Management cited pressure from memory and storage costs. How often customers use the AI features, and how profitable those particular computers are, remain open questions.

Autodesk ties its AI plans to engineering project data

Design software helps people plan things they intend to build. An AI tool working inside that software can use information from the project, much as a colleague can give a more relevant answer after reading the drawings. Autodesk, which makes software for design, engineering and construction, says its strategy combines AI with established engineering tools and more project information to improve automation. The company reported quarterly revenue of US$2.16 billion on November 24, up 17% from a year earlier. How much of that growth came from AI remains unknown, as does how reliably its tools will handle real engineering decisions.
