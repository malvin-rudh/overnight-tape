Debt-cycle read

Applies Ray Dalio's published debt-cycle framework to today's data. Not his view, and not endorsed by him.

The mechanics: A price is the money and credit spent divided by the quantity sold. Gold competes with cash and bonds as a store of wealth, so it gains when their real return falls and loses while real yields stay high and money stays tight. In a scramble for cash it can also be sold simply because it is easy to sell.

What the data say: Three forces. First, the real yield, the Treasury yield after inflation, is 2.60%: bonds still pay well, which works against gold. Second, inflation runs hot, with CPI at 3.7%, core PCE at 3.1% and labour costs up 0.8% in Q3, so markets still price hikes. Third, hiring is weak at +29k. The template fits a tight-money squeeze. The difference is Friday: real yields fell 3bp and gold fell 3.5% anyway (Kitco, 30 Oct), a forced-selling episode the template does not explain. These are rough gauges.

What looks unsustainable: Interest takes 21.9% of federal revenue, like a family whose interest bill eats a fifth of its pay.

What would change this read: October payrolls at 21:30 HKT on Fri 6 Nov, or US strikes on Iran, reported as possible from Wed 4 Nov.

Where this sits in the cycle: Debt keeps growing faster than the revenue that services it: 6.52 times revenue, against 6.40 a year ago. That fits a late, highly indebted phase. The cushion between borrowing costs and growth has thinned sharply. The 10-year now sits only 0.62 points below nominal growth, against 3.32 points a year ago. As that cushion goes, debt stops shrinking relative to income. These are rough, noisy gauges, not timing signals.

What the framework implies for asset classes: In this framework, while real yields stay positive and money is tight, cash and bonds pay a real return and gold struggles. Debt service may push policy makers toward easing or money creation; the Fed balance sheet's swing from -6.3% to +2.4% a year hints at that. If it happens, cash and bonds tend to lose buying power and gold tends to gain. Stocks depend more on productivity and earnings.

AI and tech

Intel's weak outlook triggers its worst day in decades

Gross margin is simply what is left of each sales dollar after paying the direct cost of making the product. Intel guided fourth-quarter gross margin to 38%, down from 44.1% in the third quarter. Revenue guidance was $15 billion to $16 billion against about $17.1 billion expected. Shares closed down 28.6% on 30 October, which Decrypt called the worst one-day drop in more than 50 years. The CFO said positive free cash flow next year is probably not coming, calling 2027 an investment year. Reported causes are an Israel fab shutdown after an accident and a substrate shortage, from a single source. How long either lasts is unknown.

Sources: Reuters via Investing.com · Decrypt · TIKR

OpenAI reportedly cuts its fundraising target to $20 billion

A funding round is a company selling new shares to raise cash, and the price tag is the valuation. The Financial Times reported that OpenAI cut its target from about $30 billion to about $20 billion, and its valuation from $1.4 trillion to $1.25 trillion, counted before the new money goes in. UAE investors lead, and a later offering to wealthy individuals could lift the total to $20 billion. The Wall Street Journal reported $11 billion secured so far, with some large investors tepid, though that is a single source. OpenAI had not commented, every figure is a press report, and the final size and closing date are unknown.

Sources: CoinDesk · TechCrunch · Fortune

S&P turns its outlook on Meta negative over AI spending

A credit rating is a grade for how safely a borrower can repay debt, and an outlook is a warning about which way it may move. S&P kept Meta at AA- but changed the outlook from stable to negative, which is not a downgrade. The worry is uncertainty over heavy AI spending: Meta guided capital spending of $135 billion to $145 billion. S&P said leverage could pass its 1.0x threshold as soon as 2027, against about 0.1x in 2025. Meta replied that it respectfully disagrees about its AI monetization capabilities. Whether the spending earns its cost back is the disputed point, and sources conflict on S&P's 2026 deficit forecast.

Sources: Reuters via KFGO · Fortune

Apple Intelligence comes to China, powered by Alibaba's Qwen

Behind a smart assistant sits a language model, the part that reads and writes text, and for China that part will be Alibaba's Qwen. Apple and Alibaba announced that Apple Intelligence features arrive in China, with Qwen built into Siri, Writing Tools and Visual Intelligence. Developer beta starts 4 November, and public release comes with iOS 27.3 in December for iPhone 15 Pro and later. The new Siri shown at WWDC 2026 is not included. Alibaba says it will not use user requests or responses to train its models. China's internet regulator cleared the arrangement in July. Financial terms are undisclosed, and how the features perform on a different underlying model is untested publicly.

Sources: Apple Newsroom · CGTN · The Next Web
