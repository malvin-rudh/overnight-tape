Debt-cycle read

Applies Ray Dalio's published debt-cycle framework to today's data. Not his view, and not endorsed by him.

The mechanics: Gold pays no interest. Lower after-inflation bond returns reduce its opportunity cost; a weaker dollar also makes it cheaper outside the US.

What the data say: Three forces: the latest real yield is still 2.65%, a headwind; CPI inflation of 3.74% strains purchasing power; breakeven inflation fell to 2.26%, suggesting less expected inflation. These observations cover different dates, so they cannot prove that real yields fell during the rally.

What looks unsustainable: Persistently financing debt faster than income grows eventually forces spending cuts, restructuring, transfers or monetary easing. Today’s political result alone does not establish which lever will dominate.

What would change this read: US productivity and labour costs, Thu 21:30 HKT, test inflation pressure. Payrolls, Fri 21:30 HKT, test labour strength.

Where this sits in the cycle: The indicators suggest a highly indebted phase with less room for fiscal mistakes, rather than a dated prediction of crisis. Like a family devoting a large share of income to interest, refinancing costs constrain other choices. Debt relative to revenue increased over the year, while growth in foreign Treasury holdings slowed; the script’s mixed-period ratios are rough gauges, not a clean national cash-flow account.

What the framework implies for asset classes: In the framework, positive real yields reward the purchasing power of cash and bonds and compete with gold. If financing pressure later forces easier money and currency dilution, that channel can favour gold; stocks depend on productivity and profits against financing costs. Today’s reversal in the dollar does not prove that such a monetary transition has started. Unlike a debt crisis met with monetary easing, this case still has positive real yields competing with gold; the election result does not establish a shift to financing through newly created money.

AI and tech

AI customers rise while their builders fall

Building computers for AI creates two sides of a bill: large technology companies pay for capacity, while data centre operators earn money providing it. Think of a restaurant delaying an expansion: it keeps cash, but its builder loses expected work. After Democrats won both chambers of Congress, Microsoft, Amazon and Alphabet shares rose while several data centre operators fell. Reuters attributed the split to expectations that slower construction could ease big technology companies’ spending while hurting operators and suppliers. Opposition in Texas and Georgia adds uncertainty. But these price moves reflect expectations about possible regulation, not evidence of newly enacted restrictions or cancelled projects.

Sources: Reuters · AP

Cohere hosts rival models in private vaults

A company using an AI model sends it information and receives an answer. Cohere’s Model Vault offers a private place to do that on dedicated computing infrastructure, now with other companies’ open models alongside its own. Think of renting a separate kitchen rather than sharing the counter. By default, Cohere says it does not retain prompts or answers. Its Encrypted option adds confidential computing, protection for data while the computer processes it, which Cohere says prevents even the host or Cohere from seeing those exchanges. Those security claims lack independent confirmation in the available sources. Customers pay per GPU hour, and the Encrypted option is currently available only in the US.
